  Top 5 for writing, outside the Anthropic family

  These are the best non-Claude models/tools for documentation, notes, and

  for fiction/books.
  2. OpenAI GPT (latest GPT-5.x class) — the strongest general-purpose
  alternative to Opus for nonfiction docs, structure, and editing. Reliable,
  fast, good at technical writing.
  3. Google Gemini (2.x Pro/Ultra) — huge context, strong at research-grounded
  nonfiction and pulling from source material; good for documentation that has
  to stay faithful to inputs.
  4. NovelAI — specialist novelist tool with its own tuned models, built around

  5. DeepSeek / Mistral (open-weight tier) — the best of the open models if you
  want to self-host or avoid per-token vendor lock; weaker voice than the above
  but capable for notes and drafting.

  If your priority is prose quality, the ranking is Sudowrite > GPT ≈ Gemini. If

  On the "office agent" and authoring harnesses

  What you're remembering is Claude's file-creation feature (Sept 2025) — Claude
  writes and runs its own code in a sandbox to produce real .docx, .pptx,
  .xlsx, and .pdf files, plus native Word/Excel/PowerPoint add-ins with shared
  context across all three apps. That's the "office" thing. But note: it's an
  Anthropic product, and it's a document-production harness (formatting, decks,
  spreadsheets), not a writing-craft harness. It won't help you draft a book the
  way Claude Code helps you write software.

  The real analog to "Claude Code, but for authoring" is the purpose-built 
  writing harnesses, and these are the ones worth knowing:

  - NovelCrafter — the strongest structural analog to Claude Code for books.
  Manuscript organization, codex/wiki for characters and worldbuilding,
  scene-by-scene drafting, and bring-your-own-key so you can point it at any
  model (including a non-code Claude). This is probably what you actually want:
  the harness does the discovery/continuity scaffolding (like Claude Code does
  for a codebase), and you don't burn context on it.
  - Sudowrite — more of an opinionated creative-writing studio than an open
  harness, but its tooling around "Story Bible," rewrites, and continuity is the

  - WordWriter — nonfiction-focused, with a research agent that does

  Bottom line: there's no non-code "Claude Code for prose" from Anthropic

● Yes — that's the one. Claude Cowork is Anthropic's generalist agentic harness:
  same Opus underneath, but the system prompt and tooling are built around
  doing real work on your computer and connected apps (files, browser, Slack,
  scheduled recurring tasks) instead of discovering and editing a codebase. It's
  the non-code sibling to Claude Code you were reaching for.

  How it maps to your three use cases:

  - Documentation — strong fit. It can pull from scattered sources, format
  reports, build and maintain doc files, and run that on a schedule (e.g.
  "regenerate the docs digest every Monday"). This is squarely what it's
  optimized for.
  - Notes — strong fit. Capturing, organizing, extracting, and reshaping notes
  into reports is exactly its task profile. The connectors (Slack, Chrome,
  files) make it good at gathering notes, not just writing them.
  - Book / long-form writing — weak fit, and worth being honest about. Cowork is
  a task-execution harness, not a writing-craft harness. It has no manuscript
  model — no codex of characters, no scene/continuity scaffolding, no "hold the
  whole book in view and keep voice consistent." It'll happily produce and
  format a chapter file, but it won't help you with the craft the way
  NovelCrafter (pointed at Opus) would.

  So the clean answer to what you actually want:

  - Docs + notes → Cowork. It's the right harness, it's Opus, and it won't burn
  your context on code-discovery system prompts. This is the upgrade over plain
  chat.
  - Book → still a writing-specific harness (NovelCrafter via BYO-key on Opus,
  or Sudowrite for fiction). Cowork doesn't replace that.

  One harness won't cover all three well — Cowork owns the documentation/notes
  side, a writing harness owns the book side.

● That's a strong thesis, and the title carries it — "Agents Are People Too"
  lands because the whole argument is that we keep rediscovering, in software,
  the institutional solutions humanity already worked out for fallible
  self-interested actors. The book is really about isomorphism: agent
  architecture recapitulates constitutional design.

  The spine you've already got, named precisely:

  - "A programmer can't review its own code" → this is the separation of powers
  (Montesquieu) and auditor independence and the principal-agent problem (Jensen
  & Meckling) all at once. The deepest version is Madison, Federalist 51:
  "ambition must be made to counteract ambition." You don't trust virtue; you
  structure incentives so no single actor can both act and absolve itself.
  - "It can't plan the work because it spends context" → this is bounded 
  rationality (Herbert Simon) and the division of labor (Smith). Cognition is a
  scarce resource; you specialize roles because no agent has the bandwidth to be
  sovereign over the whole.
  - "Biased by its own decision-justification" → motivated reasoning / 
  commitment bias, and institutionally, why the prosecutor isn't the judge. The
  reviewer must be structurally disinterested.
  - "Multiple agents over several lenses" → Arrow's impossibility and jury 
  theory (Condorcet): aggregating diverse imperfect judgments beats any single
  oracle, but only under independence — correlated agents are just one agent
  wearing hats.

  The thinkers that map cleanly onto your project, beyond the ones you named:

  - Hurwicz / Maskin / Myerson — mechanism design, incentive compatibility: can 
  you design the game so honesty is the dominant strategy? This is your formal
  backbone.
  - Coase & Williamson — transaction-cost economics: why do firms exist? Where 
  are the boundaries of an agent? Directly answers "when do I split one agent
  into two."
  - Elinor Ostrom — governing the commons / polycentric governance: how
  independent actors self-govern shared resources without a central planner.
  This is your multi-agent coordination chapter.
  - Max Weber — bureaucracy and roles: authority as a property of the office,
  not the person. "Playing roles" is pure Weber.
  - Schelling — conflict, coordination, credible commitment.

  The intellectual risk to manage early: the book could read as "here are some
  clever analogies." The thing that makes it real is the reverse claim — not
  just "agents are like institutions," but "the failure modes are the same 
  failure modes, and the centuries-old fixes are load-bearing, not decorative."
  When an agent reviews its own work, it fails for the same structural reason a
  self-auditing treasury fails. That's the load-bearing argument.
